Bajnok–Beatty Conjectures 4.1 and 1.6 (arXiv:2406.04045v1, Section 4, p. 10; Section 1, p. 3): classification and extremal noncyclic order

Haobo Ma, Wenlin Zhang

Paper

How large can a finite system of positions be if two reversible moves must reach every position within a fixed number of steps? Haobo Ma and Wenlin Zhang answer this for one family of commutative groups and determine the largest noncyclic finite commutative group admitting such navigation, settling two conjectures of Bajnok and Beatty.

A group describes positions, with addition describing movement. Commutativity means move order does not matter; cyclicity means one suitable move alone generates every position. Two distinct elements a and b are an s-spanning pair if every position is xa + yb with |x| + |y| ≤ s; negative coefficients mean reverse moves. Equivalently, the associated undirected movement graph has diameter at most s. The problem balances network size against maximum travel distance.

The first family consists of pairs of residues modulo 2 and modulo 2k, giving 4k positions. For positive k and s, the authors establish this complete classification: for odd s, a pair exists exactly when 2k ≤ s² − 1. For even s, every case with 2k ≤ s² − s works, and none with 2k > s² works. In the intervening interval, k must be even if s is divisible by four, and must have remainder two modulo four otherwise. Bajnok and Beatty had already proved the size bound and lower ranges; the new step completes the even-radius upper interval.

The proof studies integer coefficient pairs rather than positions. Differences between pairs reaching the same position form a repeating grid, called the relation lattice. Spanning means that translates of the coefficient diamond |x| + |y| ≤ s cover every integer point. Using the lattice viewpoint of Dougherty and Faber, the authors change coordinates to turn diamonds into squares. The group’s parity structure makes an s-by-s integer square represent all 2k classes of a transformed lattice, yielding 2k ≤ s².

The decisive observation is rigidity near equality. If the square contains fewer than s extra points beyond the number of classes, the authors prove that a lattice basis has two off-axis displacements whose product equals this deficit. Write s = 2t and k = 2t² − r, with 0 ≤ r < t. Parity forces r to be even, and divisible by four when t is odd. Conversely, explicit relation lattices produce the required groups and cover every class within the permitted distance. Thus the arithmetic conditions establish existence as well as impossibility.

For the broader problem, let N(s) denote the largest order of a noncyclic finite abelian group with an s-spanning pair. For s ≥ 3, the authors prove N(s) = 2s² if 2s + 1 is prime; otherwise N(s) = 2s² + 2s − (p² − 1)/2, where p is its smallest prime divisor. Every candidate has residue factors modulo c and ck, with c ≥ 2. Relations preserve coefficient residues modulo c, so each of the c² residue classes must contain at least k diamond points. Counting sparse classes gives upper bounds; exceeding 2s² requires the exceptional alignment c dividing 2s + 1. Bajnok and Beatty’s earlier constructions attain the bounds. The argument removes earlier restrictions on the generating pair’s form. Separately, N(2) = 9, while radius one admits no noncyclic example.

A perfect pair gives exactly one permitted coefficient pair for every position. The authors prove its group must be cyclic, but leave its full proposed shape classification unresolved. Their example {2,10} modulo 13 is perfect at radius two: zero, single, repeated and mixed moves give thirteen distinct residues. It equals five times {2,3}, but no multiplier from zero through four produces it. This corrects the conjecture’s printed multiplier range to invertible residues modulo the actual group order; correcting that range does not prove the remaining assertion.

The manuscript presents written proofs. Its reported Lean evidence covers the first-family classification, near-square lattice rigidity, necessary upper-interval congruences and the sufficient construction family—not separate statements of the global maximum or perfect-pair results. Reported exhaustive checks cover the first family through k = 40, comparing radii through 12, and all noncyclic two-generator abelian groups of order at most 61 for radii two through five. These finite checks supplement the unbounded proofs. The contribution is arithmetic rigidity behind nearly maximal two-move systems; the corrected perfect-pair classification and exact-order questions with three or more generators remain outside what this note establishes.
